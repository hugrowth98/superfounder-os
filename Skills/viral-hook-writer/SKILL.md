## What This Skill Does

Writes the first 1-3 lines of a LinkedIn post, the part people see before they decide to tap "see more." This is the single highest-leverage piece of any LinkedIn post. A great hook with an average post outperforms a great post with a weak hook every time.

Built for people who sell expertise. Not for meme accounts, not for personal branding fluff, not for engagement farming. The goal is to attract the RIGHT readers: people who have the problem you solve and the budget to hire you (`son ICP (voir `Contexte/Clients-Problems-and-Messages.md`)`).

---

## The "See More" Rule

On LinkedIn, only the first ~210 characters (roughly 2-3 short lines) are visible before the "see more" button. Everything depends on those characters. If the hook doesn't create enough tension, curiosity, or recognition to earn the click, the rest of the post doesn't exist.

A hook has ONE job: make the right person stop scrolling and tap "see more."

It does NOT need to:
- Summarize the post
- Be clever or witty
- Appeal to everyone
- Include hashtags or emojis

## Hook DNA: The 7 Patterns That Work

Every high-performing hook uses at least one of these patterns. Most use 2-3 combined.

### 1. THE PAIN MIRROR
Opens with a situation your ICP is currently living. They read it and think "that's me."

**Pattern:** [Describe their exact situation in 1-2 lines]

**Examples:**
- "You've been posting on LinkedIn for 6 months. Zero inbound leads."
- "Your referral pipeline dried up and you're pretending it didn't."
- "You know you're good at what you do. You just can't get anyone to notice."

**Why it works:** Recognition is the most powerful scroll-stopper. When someone sees their own reality described accurately, they HAVE to keep reading.

**Best for:** Teaching posts, framework posts, case studies

### 2. THE PATTERN INTERRUPT
Says something unexpected or counterintuitive that breaks the reader's assumptions.

**Pattern:** [Statement that contradicts common belief]

**Examples:**
- "The best LinkedIn content strategy is to post less."
- "Your content isn't the problem. Your offer is."
- "I stopped trying to go viral. My revenue doubled."

**Why it works:** Cognitive dissonance. The brain can't scroll past something that contradicts what it believes. It needs resolution.

**Best for:** Opinion posts, contrarian takes, belief-shift content

### 3. THE SPECIFIC NUMBER
Leads with a concrete, specific metric that signals real results.

**Pattern:** [Number] + [specific context] + [timeframe]

**Examples:**
- "12 inbound leads in 30 days. From a LinkedIn profile with 800 followers."
- "One post. 47 DMs. 6 calls booked. Here's what I did differently."
- "$4,500. That's what one LinkedIn post generated last Tuesday."

**Why it works:** Specificity = credibility. "I got great results" is ignorable. "$4,500 from one post" is not.

**Best for:** Case studies, results posts, proof-of-concept content

### 4. THE OPEN LOOP
Creates a gap between what the reader knows and what they want to know. The only way to close it is to keep reading.

**Pattern:** [Tease a result or insight] + [withhold the how/what]

**Examples:**
- "I changed one line on my LinkedIn profile. Got 3 DMs within 48 hours."
- "A client told me something last week that completely changed how I think about pricing."
- "There's a reason your best posts don't generate leads. And it's not what you think."

**Why it works:** The brain hates incomplete information. An open loop creates tension that can only be resolved by reading more.

**Best for:** Story posts, lesson posts, framework reveals

### 5. THE DIRECT ADDRESS
Calls out a specific person. Uses "you" or names the ICP explicitly. Feels like the post was written FOR them.

**Pattern:** "If you're a [ICP] who [situation]..."

**Examples:**
- "If you're a consultant making $10K/month from referrals and wondering what happens when they dry up, read this."
- "Founders with 500-2,000 followers: this post is for you."
- "You're an expert. You've got 15 years of experience. And your LinkedIn is a ghost town."

**Why it works:** Specificity creates belonging. When you name someone's exact situation, they feel seen, and they lean in.

**Best for:** Any post type. Especially effective for handraiser and teaching posts.

### 6. THE "STOP DOING THIS"
Identifies a common mistake and calls it out directly. Not shaming, correcting.

**Pattern:** [Common behavior] + [why it's costing them]

**Examples:**
- "Stop writing LinkedIn posts for other LinkedIn creators. They're not going to hire you."
- "Your headline says 'Helping businesses grow.' That's why nobody DMs you."
- "If your CTA is 'like and share if you agree', you're optimizing for the wrong metric."

**Why it works:** Loss aversion. People are more motivated to stop doing something wrong than to start doing something right.

**Best for:** Teaching posts, myth-busting, opinion content

### 7. THE STORY ENTRY
Drops the reader into a specific moment. No preamble, no context. Just action.

**Pattern:** [Specific moment in time] + [sensory or emotional detail]

**Examples:**
- "Tuesday, 9:14 AM. I opened LinkedIn and saw 4 unread DMs. All from people I'd never spoken to."
- "She said: 'I've been doing this for 12 years and nobody knows I exist.' That hit different."
- "'I just closed a $5,000 client from a LinkedIn post.' My client sent me that voice note at 11pm."

**Why it works:** Stories bypass the analytical brain. A specific moment creates an image, and images create curiosity.

**Best for:** Story posts, client win posts, personal narrative

## Hook Quality Checklist

Before finalizing a hook, verify:

- [ ] **Specific, not vague.** "$4,500" not "great results." "30 days" not "quickly."
- [ ] **Creates tension.** There's a gap, a contradiction, a question, or a moment of recognition that demands resolution.
- [ ] **Under 210 characters.** Ideally under 150. Short hooks outperform long ones.
- [ ] **No wasted words.** Every word earns its place. Cut "I think that" and "In my experience" and "Let me share."
- [ ] **Attracts the RIGHT reader.** Your ICP reads this and stops. Random LinkedIn scrollers keep going. That's the goal.
- [ ] **No clickbait.** The post must deliver on whatever the hook promises. A hook that overpromises and underdelivers kills trust.
- [ ] **No emojis in the hook.** They cheapen the opening and signal "content creator" not "expert."
- [ ] **Line 1 does the heavy lifting.** If someone only reads the first line, is there enough to make them read line 2?

## What NOT to Do

**Don't open with a question everyone can answer:**
- Bad: "Want more clients?" (Yes. Obviously. So does everyone. This filters nobody.)
- Good: "Your referral pipeline dried up 6 months ago. Here's what to do about it."

**Don't open with "I":**
- Bad: "I've been thinking a lot about content strategy lately."
- Good: "Most content strategies are backwards. Here's why."

**Don't use LinkedIn clichés:**
- Bad: "Agree? 👇" / "Hot take:" / "Unpopular opinion:" / "I'm going to say something controversial..."
- Good: Just say the thing. The hook IS the hot take.

**Don't front-load your credentials:**
- Bad: "As a certified expert with 20 years of experience..."
- Good: Save credentials for the body. The hook is about THEM, not you.

**Don't write hooks that could be about anything:**
- Bad: "Success isn't about working harder. It's about working smarter."
- Good: "I cut my posting from 5x/week to 3x/week. Inbound leads went up 40%."

## Output Format

When delivering hooks, present them like this:

```
**Hook 1** (Pain Mirror + Direct Address)
[Hook text - 1-3 lines]
→ Why it works: [1 sentence]

**Hook 2** (Specific Number + Open Loop)
[Hook text - 1-3 lines]
→ Why it works: [1 sentence]

**Hook 3** (Pattern Interrupt)
[Hook text - 1-3 lines]
→ Why it works: [1 sentence]
```

Always provide at least 3 variations using different patterns so the user can pick the one that fits their voice and comfort level. If they provide a draft hook, also include a rewrite of their original with notes on what changed and why.
